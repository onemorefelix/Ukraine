Part One

Land, Symbols & Places

Kyivan Rus roots, Cossack character, and the black soil that grows both wheat and an emblem a thousand years old.

01 / 11

Capital, People & Regions

  • Kyiv, on the Dnipro River
  • 38–41 million people — give or take
  • 24 oblasts, plus Crimea

Nobody has a clean number for the population anymore — not carelessness, just the toll of a hard decade. The map, at least, hasn’t moved: 24 oblasts, same as it’s been for years, plus Crimea’s separate status.

02 / 11

The Landscape — Steppe to Carpathians

  • Flat steppe in the south
  • Dense forest up north
  • The Carpathians climbing the west

Drive west long enough and the horizon just stops being flat. That’s the Carpathians announcing themselves — the highest ground in the country, and the payoff for the whole trip.

Where to go: the Carpathians, up to Mount Hoverla (2,061 m)

03 / 11

Chornozem — the Black Soil

  • Some of the richest soil on Earth
  • The real reason for “breadbasket of Europe”

Chornozem isn’t a nickname, it’s a soil type — dark, humus-rich, absurdly fertile — and it covers enough of the country that Ukrainian wheat still moves world markets.

04 / 11

Kyivan Rus & the Tryzub

  • Kyivan Rus, 9th–13th century
  • Where the tryzub begins

This brick is about a thousand years old, and that three-pronged mark carved into it started as nothing grander than a prince’s personal seal — Volodymyr the Great’s, among others — long before anyone called it a national symbol.

Where to go: St. Sophia Cathedral, the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra — both UNESCO-listed

05 / 11

The Cossack Era & the Zaporozhian Sich

  • A self-governing stronghold on the Dnipro
  • Freedom-first, by design

The Zaporozhian Sich was running its own affairs on Khortytsia island long before “self-determination” was a phrase anyone needed. This is where the stubborn streak in the Ukrainian character actually starts.

06 / 11

1986 & 1991 — Chornobyl to Independence

  • 1986 — Chornobyl
  • 1991 — independence

No politics, just the arc: a scar in ’86, and — improbably, via the video game S.T.A.L.K.E.R. — a strange cultural afterlife that introduced the Zone to people who’d never otherwise have heard of it. Five years later, independence.

Where to go: Kamianets-Podilskyi, one of the best-preserved Cossack-era fortresses

07 / 11

The Tryzub & the Flag

  • A 1,000-year-old emblem
  • Blue over yellow: sky over wheat
  • Folk reading: three prongs spelling “ВОЛЯ”

The flag never needed a redesign — sky over a wheat field, the same landscape this whole page keeps returning to. The tryzub picked up its own legend along the way: read the prongs right, some say, and they spell “ВОЛЯ” — freedom. No historian will back that up. Doesn’t stop anyone believing it anyway.

08 / 11

The Vyshyvanka — Embroidered Identity

  • A traditional embroidered shirt
  • No two villages stitch it the same

Cross a valley and the pattern changes completely — that specific. And it’s not a museum piece: people still wear these on an ordinary Tuesday, not just for the cameras.

09 / 11

Taras Shevchenko & the Kobzar

  • Poet, painter, national icon
  • Kobzar, the book everyone knows

Shevchenko matters enough that a monument to him stands outside the Manitoba Legislature in Winnipeg — unveiled in 1961, a century after his death, on the opposite side of the planet from anywhere he ever set foot.

Where to go: Lviv, for the architecture and the coffee

10 / 11

Pysanky — History & Technique

  • Stone and ceramic versions trace to Kyivan Rus
  • The technique: wax, dye, repeat

It’s batik, essentially — wax drawn on in careful lines, colour built up layer by layer, lightest first, and the wax finally melted away to reveal a pattern that was there the whole time, just hidden.

11 / 11

Pysanky — Color & the Giant Egg

  • Red for life, yellow for harvest, green for spring
  • Handed out at Easter
  • Its own Canadian coin since 2016

Every colour is already telling the story before anyone opens their mouth — and Vegreville, Canada took that literally, building a pysanka the size of a small building in 1974. Still the largest one on Earth. The Royal Canadian Mint joined in too, striking an egg-shaped silver coin every year since 2016 that sells out almost on contact.

Where to go: Kosiv, in the Hutsul Carpathians

Part Two

Mythology, Holidays & Music

The spirits that still live behind the stove, the fires lit at midsummer, and the instruments that never stopped playing in the mountains.

01 / 11

Domovyk — the House Spirit

  • Lives behind the stove
  • Keeps your luck, if you keep him fed

Feed him. Respect him. That’s the whole arrangement with Domovyk. Break it, and nothing dramatic happens — the luck just quietly, deliberately, turns. You won’t get a warning first.

02 / 11

Mavka — the Forest Maiden

  • Forest spirit, beautiful, not entirely safe
  • Beauty enough to pull you off the path

Don’t follow her off the trail after dark. She’s lovelier than she has any right to be, and that’s rather the point — Mavka’s origin traces back to the souls of children who died too soon, which makes her less a monster than a sad story wearing a gorgeous shape.

03 / 11

Rusalka — the Water Spirit

  • Water spirit, tied to rivers and lakes
  • Most active during Rusalka Week, early summer

Kramskoi painted these in 1871 — nineteen rusalky drifting by moonlight, right when artists everywhere were turning back to village ghost stories for material. One tradition has never really faded: don’t go swimming alone during Rusalka Week. Not even now.

04 / 11

Berehynia & the Horseshoe

  • Mother-protector of the family line
  • The horseshoe over the door: the same idea, sized down

She stands over the entire country, literally — twenty tonnes of bronze on a column sixty-one metres tall, dead centre of Kyiv’s Independence Square since 2001. Every horseshoe nailed above a doorway is the same protective instinct, just small enough to fit in your hand.

05 / 11

Kalyna — Blood, Family & Homeland

  • Bright red viburnum berries
  • A fixture in Ukrainian song

It’s doing three jobs at once and nobody bothers separating them out — kalyna reads as blood, as family, and as homeland, all in the same red berry, and it shows up in more songs than almost anything else in the tradition.

06 / 11

The Stork — New Life on the Roof

  • Home, family, new life
  • A nest on the roof: a good sign

The logic is almost sweetly literal: a stork’s nest on your roof means a new life is headed for that house. Plenty of rural families still leave an old cart wheel up there — a little encouragement, just in case.

07 / 11

Ivana Kupala — Wreaths & Fire

  • Night of July 6–7, the summer solstice
  • Wreaths on the water; a bonfire jumped hand in hand

Two fortune-telling rituals, one night. A wreath set adrift reads a girl’s romantic future in how it drifts away from her. A couple leaps the bonfire together, hands locked — let go mid-air, and everyone watching knows exactly what that means. The water that night looks calm. That’s precisely when you’re told not to trust it.

08 / 11

Sviatvechir — the Twelve Dishes

  • Christmas Eve, twelve meatless dishes
  • Kutia, sweet wheat porridge, front and centre

Twelve isn’t decorative — one dish per apostle, which quietly turns an ordinary family dinner into a full piece of theology nobody has to explain out loud.

09 / 11

Vertep & Easter — Closing the Loop

  • Vertep — puppets retell the Nativity, mostly for laughs
  • Easter — back to the pysanky this page opened with

Vertep is folk theatre, not solemn ritual — puppets doing the Nativity with a wink. Easter is the quieter close: the same painted eggs from the start of this page, handed out again as a promise that things come back.

10 / 11

Hopak & the Bandura

  • Hopak — acrobatic, Cossack-rooted
  • Bandura — 60-plus strings, built for storytelling

A full-sized bandura carries sixty or more strings, and that’s no accident — it made the instrument for the kobzars, the travelling bards who kept Ukrainian history alive in songs long before anyone bothered writing much of it down.

11 / 11

Sopilka, Tsymbaly & the Trembita

  • Sopilka — a wooden flute older than the written record here
  • Tsymbaly and the long Hutsul trembita — built to carry

The trembita runs several metres long, and mountain villages still play it at weddings and funerals alike — not a performance for visitors, just the sound that’s always carried from one ridge to the next when it matters.
